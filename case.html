Case study · HBO's Succession (2018–2023)

Six ways the Roys got it wrong, and what a family table would have changed.

Succession is four seasons about one question: who takes over when the founder sells? We used it as a case study, checked each pattern against PNC's own succession papers and outside research, and built our personas from it.

Why a TV show?

Every judge and advisor has seen it, so it explains the problem in seconds. And every failure in it shows up in PNC's research on real family-business exits. What the Roys never had was one place where the whole family could be heard, and one advisor who could see all of it.

The six patterns

1

The promise made in private

In Succession

Logan quietly tells Shiv she will take over in a few years (S2, “The Summer Palace”). Kendall says he was told as a boy that he would be CEO. Several children each believe they are the one.

In real life

PNC's papers warn that families think they're aligned until a deal forces the question. Julius Baer finds 37% of wealth-transfer talks still happen informally.

Family Table

Private check-ins catch the collision: 3 of 5 expect to lead. Alex sees the pattern, not the names, and brings it to a facilitated meeting. See it

2

The founder who won't step back

In Succession

On his 80th birthday Logan backs out of retiring, then suffers a health scare the same day (S1, “Celebration”).

In real life

Owners struggle to face an exit and underestimate how much the business depends on them. 75% of owners regretted selling a year later (PNC).

Family Table

Walt's screen leads with what he keeps and his next chapter, so life after the sale is planned before he signs. See it

3

The clause nobody remembered

In Succession

The children's power to block the sale comes from their parents' old divorce agreement. Logan rewrites it with their mother behind their backs (S3, “All the Bells Say”).

In real life

PNC advises reviewing buy-sell agreements and bylaws early; buyers find surprises in diligence and use them to renegotiate.

Family Table

Document AI flags the 1998 consent clause months before closing. An attorney confirms it, and all four children are told. See it

4

The heir who just needs cash

In Succession

Connor, the eldest and outside the business, keeps circling back to money for his own plans while his siblings fight over control.

In real life

Children not in the business often expect equal shares; equal ownership between active and non-active children causes conflict (PNC).

Family Table

Dale's screen leads with his share and when it arrives. A slider shows how each split changes everyone's number. See it

5

The death in the middle of the deal

In Succession

Logan dies on a plane while the sale is still being negotiated (S4, “Connor's Wedding”). Nobody knows who is in charge.

In real life

Half of business exits are involuntary: death, disability, divorce, disagreement or distress (PNC).

Family Table

A signed “if Walt can't continue” plan, agreed up front: who leads for 90 days, and how the family decides. See it

6

The note in the safe

In Succession

After Logan dies, the family finds an undated note naming Kendall. Nobody can agree whether his name is underlined or crossed out (S4, “Honeymoon States”).

In real life

Undocumented intentions are how families end up in court. PNC's papers stress defining success in writing for every stakeholder.

Family Table

Document AI marks the desk note as not valid. Every decision goes in a dated, signed log that PNC keeps. See it

How the show ends

The sale goes through and the son-in-law becomes CEO; none of the children get the company (S4, “With Open Eyes”). The family is rich and broken. PNC's own definition of a good exit is the opposite: it's not what you walk away with, but how well your life works after.

What this means for the design

Privacy is the product

Patterns 1 and 4 only show up if people can speak without the founder watching. So answers are private by default, and the advisor sees gaps, not words.

Paper beats memory

Patterns 3, 5 and 6 are about documents: old, missing or ambiguous. So Document AI reads the old ones and the decision log replaces the new ones.

Each person needs a different screen

Dale wants a number. Kyle wants a role. Walt wants a legacy. So the app adapts to each person, and says why.
