The case: Wilson Transport, Pittsburgh

Five moments where a family sale goes wrong, and what each side needs.

Wilson Transport is a fictional $48M trucking company with 240 staff, sized for PNC's commercial clients. Each moment below is drawn from PNC's own succession research, and each one is a screen in the prototype.

The Wilsons

One founder, one advisor, and the three people whose futures depend on the split. Everyone here is fictional.

MW

Margaret Wilson, 63

Founder and CEO. Existing PNC commercial client
Wants
A clear view of what the family can spend, gift and invest; a split that feels fair to every child; purpose after the sale.
Believes
I think the kids are on the same page.
Sees first
What she keeps at closing, then an invitation to bring her family in.
AL

Alex Lee, PNC

Relationship Manager and Private Business Strategist
Wants
To engage the family before the sale closes, keep the proceeds at PNC, and bring in facilitation when it's needed.
Struggles with
Hearing only Margaret's side; specialists spread across business units; tension that surfaces after decisions are made.
Sees first
A sale signal, who has had their say, and what needs attention this week.
BW

Ben Wilson, 40

COO, 16 years in the business
Wants
His years in the company to count. He stays on with the buyer and keeps $1.5M of its equity.
Would only say privately
I've put in 16 years. That should mean something.
Sees first
His role after the sale.
CW

Claire Wilson, 37

Nurse practitioner in Columbus
Wants
Equal shares, and not to fight with her brother over money.
Would only say privately
Sam and I only get what's in the split. I've never said that to Mom.
Sees first
The family meeting, then her check-in.
SW

Sam Wilson, 33

Software engineer in Austin
Wants
To know how much, and when.
Would only say privately
I don't need a role. I need a date.
Sees first
His share under each split, and when it arrives.

Tom Wilson, 65, Margaret's husband, isn't an owner, but his future depends on the plan. He's invited and hasn't checked in yet.

Five moments that go wrong

The family that thinks it agrees

What happens

Margaret plans an equal split and assumes everyone is fine with it. Ben expects his 16 years to count. Claire thinks equal is the only fair answer. Nobody has said so out loud.

What the research says

PNC's papers warn that families think they're aligned until a deal forces the question. More than 1 in 3 owners have no formal plan, and family conflict is the most common reason (PNC).

Family Table

Private check-ins, then an anonymous summary: the widest gap is fairness, set against Margaret's equal-split plan. See it

The advisor who hears one voice

What happens

Alex talks to Margaret. The children bank elsewhere and have never met anyone at PNC. Their concerns stay undocumented until the money is already moving.

What the research says

Only 13% of adult children use their parents' financial advisor (Cerulli, 2019).

Family Table

A sale signal a year ahead, four voices instead of one, and an introduction to the heirs before $8–9M reaches them. See it

The document written for a different life

What happens

Margaret's 2012 will leaves company shares to the children. After the sale there are no shares, only cash, and the will says nothing about Ben's buyer equity. A key-person policy ends at closing.

What the research says

PNC advises reviewing buy-sell agreements and bylaws early. Buyers find surprises in due diligence and use them to renegotiate.

Family Table

Document AI flags the will and the policy a year early. Each goes to the right specialist before the family is told. See it

The heir who only needs a number

What happens

Sam doesn't care who runs anything. He wants to know how much, and when. Without a number he fills the gap with guesses, and resentment.

What the research says

Children not in the business often expect equal shares, and equal ownership between active and non-active children causes conflict (PNC).

Family Table

Sam's screen leads with his share and when it arrives. Each split option shows every child's number, and he votes in private. See it

The founder with no next chapter

What happens

Margaret has worked every day for 35 years. The sale plan covers the money, not her Monday mornings. Her children worry about it more than she does.

What the research says

75% of owners “profoundly regretted” selling a year later. Half of exits are involuntary, so a plan for the unexpected matters as much (PNC).

Margaret's plan starts with her next chapter, and a signed plan covers what happens if she can't continue. See it

What this means for the design

Privacy is the product

Moments 1 and 2 only surface if people can speak without the founder watching. So answers are private by default, and the advisor sees gaps, not words.

Paper beats memory

Moment 3 is about documents written for a different life. So Document AI reads the old ones, and a signed decision log replaces the new ones.

Each person needs a different screen

Sam wants a number. Ben wants a role. Margaret wants a plan she trusts. So the app adapts to each person, and says why.
